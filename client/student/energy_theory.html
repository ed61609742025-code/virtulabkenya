Exothermic & Endothermic Fundamentals

Thermodynamic energy balance and temperature effects

In every chemical reaction, chemical bonds in the reactants are broken (an endothermic process that absorbs energy) and new chemical bonds in the products are formed (an exothermic process that releases energy). The overall enthalpy change (ΔH) is the net difference between these two quantities.

ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants
Measured under standard conditions: 298 K (25°C) and 1 atmosphere (101.3 kPa).
🔥 Exothermic Reactions (ΔH < 0)
  • Heat energy is released to the surroundings.
  • Temperature of the reaction mixture increases (ΔT > 0).
  • Products have lower enthalpy than reactants (Hp < Hr).
  • Enthalpy change carries a negative sign (e.g. ΔH = −217.0 kJ/mol).
  • Examples: Neutralization of acids and bases, combustion of fuels, metal displacement reactions (Zn + CuSO₄), dissolving anhydrous CuSO₄ or CaCl₂.
🧊 Endothermic Reactions (ΔH > 0)
  • Heat energy is absorbed from the surroundings.
  • Temperature of the reaction mixture decreases (ΔT < 0).
  • Products have higher enthalpy than reactants (Hp > Hr).
  • Enthalpy change carries a positive sign (e.g. ΔH = +25.7 kJ/mol).
  • Examples: Dissolving Ammonium Nitrate (NH₄NO₃) or Potassium Nitrate (KNO₃) in water, photosynthesis, thermal decomposition of carbonates (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).

Reaction Coordinate Profiles & Activation Energy (Ea)

Visualizing enthalpy plateaus, transition states, and catalytic pathways

An Energy Level Diagram / Reaction Profile represents the energetic trajectory of reacting particles as they collide, form an unstable high-energy Activated Complex (Transition State ‡), and transform into products.

Interactive Enthalpy Profile Visualizer
Exothermic Trajectory: Reactants overcome the Activation Energy (Ea) barrier to reach the transition state. As products form, more energy is released in bond-making than was absorbed in bond-breaking, resulting in a net negative ΔH.
Activation Energy (Ea): The minimum quantity of kinetic energy colliding reactant particles must possess for a collision to be effective (lead to chemical reaction).
Activated Complex (‡): An unstable, short-lived, high-energy intermediate state in which old bonds are partially broken and new bonds are partially formed.
Role of a Catalyst: Provides an alternative reaction mechanism with a lower Activation Energy (E'a). It increases the reaction rate without altering the overall enthalpy change (ΔH).

Standard Enthalpy Types in KCSE Chemistry

Precise definitions, thermochemical equations, and practical aspects
1. Enthalpy of Neutralization (ΔHneut) ~ −57.1 kJ/mol

The enthalpy change that occurs when one mole of water (H₂O) is formed from the reaction between an acid and a base under standard conditions.

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)      ΔHneut = −57.1 kJ/mol
💡 KNEC Key Examiner Note: For reactions between any strong monobasic acid (HCl, HNO₃) and strong base (NaOH, KOH), ΔHneut is constant (≈ −57.1 kJ/mol) because both are completely dissociated into ions. When a weak acid (CH₃COOH) is used, ΔHneut is slightly less negative (≈ −55.2 kJ/mol) because part of the heat evolved is absorbed to completely ionize the weak acid molecules!
2. Enthalpy of Displacement (ΔHdisp) −217.0 kJ/mol (Zn + Cu²⁺)

The enthalpy change that occurs when one mole of a substance is displaced from an aqueous solution of its salt by a more reactive metal.

Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)      ΔHdisp = −217.0 kJ/mol
3. Enthalpy of Solution (ΔHsol) ΔHsol = ΔHlatt + ΔHhyd

The enthalpy change when one mole of a solute is completely dissolved in excess solvent (water) such that further dilution causes no further heat change.

NH₄NO₃(s) + aq → NH₄⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)      ΔHsol = +25.7 kJ/mol
4. Enthalpy of Combustion (ΔHc) −1368.0 kJ/mol (Ethanol)

The enthalpy change that occurs when one mole of a substance is burned completely in excess oxygen under standard conditions.

C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)      ΔHc = −1368.0 kJ/mol

Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation

Constructing energy cycles to calculate indirect enthalpy changes
📜 The Law:

"The enthalpy change accompanying a chemical reaction is independent of the pathway taken, provided the initial and final states of the reactants and products are identical."

Hess's Law is invaluable because many enthalpy changes cannot be determined directly by experiment (e.g. anhydrous CuSO₄ cannot be hydrated to form solid pentahydrate crystals without dissolving in water).

Hydration Enthalpy Energy Cycle for Copper(II) Sulfate:
CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l)
Anhydrous Salt + Water
ΔH₁ (Hydration Target — Indirect) ────────────────────────►
CuSO₄·5H₂O(s)
Hydrated Crystals
ΔH₂ (Dissolving Anhydrous) = −66.5 kJ/mol ▼
Cu²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 5H₂O(l)
Aqueous Solution State
ΔH₃ (Dissolving Hydrated) = +11.7 kJ/mol ▲
Hess's Law Calculation:
ΔH₁ = ΔH₂ − ΔH₃ = (−66.5) − (+11.7) = −78.2 kJ/mol

KNEC Paper 3 Graphing & Dual-Line Extrapolation

Why extrapolation is essential and how examiners grade candidate graphs

In any school calorimetry practical, the reaction does not happen instantaneously. As heat is released, some heat is simultaneously lost to the surroundings (conduction through the polystyrene cup walls and convection from the open top). Therefore, the highest recorded thermometer reading is always slightly lower than the true theoretical maximum.

The 4-Step KNEC Dual Extrapolation Procedure:
  1. Step 1 (Baseline): Draw a horizontal line through initial readings (0s to 120s). Extrapolate this line forward to the mixing time (t = 150s / 2.5 min).
  2. Step 2 (Cooling Curve): Draw a straight line of best fit through the cooling region points (t ≥ 210s to 360s).
  3. Step 3 (Backward Extrapolation): Extrapolate the cooling line backward to intersect the vertical line corresponding to the mixing time (t = 150s).
  4. Step 4 (Determine ΔT): The vertical distance between the extrapolated maximum temperature (Tmax, extrapolated) and the baseline temperature (Tinitial) gives the true ΔT.
Rubric Code Description Examiner Requirement Marks
Scale (S) Axes scaling Plotted area must cover ≥ 50% of the millimeter grid; simple uniform multiples (e.g. 1 cm = 5°C or 30 s). 1.0 Mk
Axes (A) Axes labeling Both axes clearly labeled with quantities and correct units: Time (seconds) on x-axis and Temperature (°C) on y-axis. 1.0 Mk
Plotting (P) Data accuracy All points plotted accurately with a small cross ('X') or dot within ± 1/2 small grid square. 1.0 Mk
Curve / Line (C) Smooth construction Clean straight lines through steady initial points and cooling points. No multiple overlapping fuzzy lines. 1.0 Mk
Extrapolation (E) Intersection at tmix Clear dashed or solid extrapolation line meeting vertically at t = 2.5 min (150 s). 1.0 Mk

Step-by-Step KCSE Paper 3 Calculation Suite

Standard formulae, unit conversions, and sign conventions
Step (a)
Determine Temperature Change (ΔT)
ΔT = Textrapolated, max − Tinitial   (°C)
Read directly from the vertical extrapolation bracket on your millimeter graph. Record to 1 decimal place.
Step (b)
Calculate Quantity of Heat Evolved (Q)
Q = m · c · ΔT   (Joules)
Assume density of solution = 1.0 g/cm³ (so 25.0 cm³ = 25.0 g) and c = 4.2 J/g/°C.
Step (c)
Calculate Moles of Limiting Reactant (n)
n = (M × V) / 1000   or   n = mass / RAM   (moles)
Always calculate moles of the limiting reactant (not the reactant in excess, such as excess Zinc powder).
Step (d)
Calculate Molar Enthalpy of Reaction (ΔH)
ΔH = ± Q / (n × 1000)   (kJ/mol)
CRITICAL KNEC PENALTY RULE: Divide by 1000 to convert Joules to kilojoules. You MUST include the sign: negative (−) for temperature rise, positive (+) for temperature drop!

⚠️ 5 Common Exam Mistakes
  • Mixing Too Early: Mixing solid before t = 2.5 min spoils the initial baseline.
  • Missing Sign on ΔH: Writing 217 kJ/mol instead of −217 kJ/mol loses full marks.
  • Units Mismatch: Forgetting to divide Q in Joules by 1000 to give ΔH in kJ/mol.
  • Inconsistent Decimals: Table temperatures must all be to 1 decimal place (e.g. 22.0, 22.5).
  • Wrong Mass in mcΔT: In neutralization (25 cm³ HCl + 25 cm³ NaOH), total volume is 50 g, not 25 g!
📐 Formula Quick Card
Heat Evolved: Q = m · c · ΔT
Solution Mass: m = V × ρ
Moles: n = (M × V) / 1000
Molar Enthalpy: ΔH = ± Q / (n × 1000)
Hess's Law: ΔH₁ = ΔH₂ − ΔH₃
